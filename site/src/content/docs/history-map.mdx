Every lesson in this course drops a date and a place in passing — "in 1967, IBM's CP-67 ran on a System/360," "on the night of October 29, 1969, at UCLA." A postmark does the same thing to a letter: one stamp, fusing a date to a place. Below are the twenty-two dated claims from these lessons that reduce to one point on a map, plus the ones that don't.

<div class="kz-note">
  <strong>The map's real finding:</strong> seven of these events — Ethernet (1973), the TCP/IP paper (1974), SSL (1994–95), cgroups (2007), the Borg paper (2014), and Meta's 2021 outage — happened within about ten miles of each other on the San Francisco peninsula. So did the far end of the very first ARPANET call, dialed from UCLA in 1969. Half a century of this course's timeline keeps landing on the same stretch of coastline.
</div>

Pins mark the best-documented institutional home for each event — a lab, a university, a company's HQ — not the exact room. It's a schematic strip of the mid-latitude northern hemisphere, not a navigational chart: longitude runs true, latitude is stretched so the pins don't crowd into a sliver.

## No fixed address

The lessons mention plenty of other dated milestones that don't reduce to one point on a map — a spec finalized by a working group, a kernel feature merged in pieces over years, a tool released by a distributed team. Real dates; no pin to put them on.
